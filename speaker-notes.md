## Slide 1 — Opening — Two Cities. Two Stories.

**Presenters:** Om + Saloni  ·  **Suggested time:** ~45s

On the right — a town perched almost two kilometres above sea level, where the mountains begin. Most people think Uttarakhand is only one story: hills, temples, tourism. But our state has two very different stories, told by two very different landscapes. Today we take you to two of its cities — and we'll show how each one answers the same question in its own way: how should people live with nature? ## Slide 2 — The Journey — From the Gangetic Plain to the High Himalaya

**Presenters:** Om + Saloni  ·  **Suggested time:** ~55s

OM: Our journey climbs more than one and a half kilometres in under 250 kilometres of road. We start in Roorkee — 274 metres above sea level, on the Doab plain between the Ganga and the Yamuna, about 30 km from Haridwar. From there the road climbs through Haridwar and Rishikesh, then follows the Alaknanda river through the prayags — Devprayag, Rudraprayag, Karnaprayag — confluence points that are themselves sacred towns. SALONI: And at the end of that climb sits my town, Joshimath — 1,890 metres up, in Chamoli district. Beyond it: Badrinath at 3,300 metres, and Auli's ski slopes. Notice the elevation strip — the road isn't just a line on a map, it's a ladder between two worlds.

Extra context: trace the route with your hand as the line draws. Name the five prayags only if time allows.

Transition: “Before we climb, let's start in the plains — with a village that history almost forgot.”

## Slide 3 — Roorkee — From a Small Village to an Engineering City

**Presenters:** Om  ·  **Suggested time:** ~60s

OM: Here is something almost nobody believes: one of India's great engineering cities began as a cluster of mud huts. Roorkee's name is old — the Mughal court's own administration record, the Ain-i-Akbari written for Emperor Akbar in the 1590s, already lists it as a pargana. In the 18th century it belonged to the Landhaura estate under Raja Ramdayal. But until the 1840s it was a quiet village — mud houses, fields, and one strategic detail: it sat exactly where any canal leaving the Ganga would have to pass. In 1842 that detail changed everything. The East India Company chose this spot as the headquarters of the Upper Ganga Canal works — the largest irrigation work attempted in India at the time. Within a decade, workshops, houses, a cantonment and a college had grown around it. The village didn't just grow — it was reborn as a company town for engineers.

Extra context: if asked, the name legends — Rurimadi Imran or “Ror ki” — are local traditions, not established history.

Transition: “And what the engineers built here was, frankly, audacious.”

Time: ~60s

---

## Slide 4 — Roorkee — The Solani Aqueduct: Water Flows Over Water

**Presenters:** Om  ·  **Suggested time:** ~70s

OM: Here is the audacious part. A canal only works if its water keeps flowing downhill — very, very gently. The Upper Ganga Canal could not afford to dip down into the Solani river valley and climb back out; the water level had to be maintained. Cautley's answer: put the canal on a bridge and carry it across the sky. That is exactly what the Solani Aqueduct does — you can see it in this aerial photograph: two rivers, one blue, one brown, crossing each other at different levels. Water flowing over water. The canal bed sits roughly 25 metres above the river, and the aqueduct carries the channel for about half a kilometre. Watch the diagram: the canal water — in canal blue — slides slowly across the top, while the Solani — in Himalayan blue — keeps its own course underneath. Built in the 1840s–50s with brick, lime and human muscle — and it is still carrying water more than 170 years later.

Extra context: Cautley had no steam-powered heavy machinery here — this was pre-railway logistics for most of the work; the earth-moving railway came later during construction. The canal opened in 1854 and was, at the time, among the largest canal systems in the world.

Transition: “A project like this needs trained engineers. Where do you get them? You build a school.”

Time: ~70s

## Slide 5 — Roorkee — The Engineering Legacy (College → IIT)

**Presenters:** Om  ·  **Suggested time:** ~65s

OM: Build a giant canal, and you must train engineers to build and maintain it. So in 1847, right beside the works, Roorkee College opened its doors — and in 1854 it was renamed Thomason College of Civil Engineering, after Lieutenant-Governor James Thomason. And here's my favourite footnote: on 22 December 1851, the world's famous 'Jenny' locomotive hauled earth for the canal along a temporary construction railway between Roorkee and Piran Kaliyar — India's first steam freight run, two years before Bombay's first passenger train. Roorkee is also home to the Bengal Sappers — the Bengal Engineer Group — which made the town its home in the 1850s; Roorkee cantonment is counted among India's oldest. The college kept growing: in 1949 it became the University of Roorkee — India's first engineering university — and in 2001 it was elevated to IIT Roorkee. One institution, over 175 years old, born directly from the canal.

Extra context: the locomotive's name appears in sources as 'Jenny Lind' (after the Swedish opera singer) or simply 'Thomason'; the key verified fact is the December 1851 freight run. The Sappers later played a major role in projects across British India.

Transition: “Engineering is only half of Roorkee's identity — the other half is how people actually live beside the water.”

Time: ~65s

## Slide 6 — Roorkee — Culture & Places Around

**Presenters:** Om  ·  **Suggested time:** ~60s

OM: If you visit Roorkee today, three places will tell you what the town really is. First — Piran Kaliyar Sharif, about 25 km away: the dargah of the 13th-century Sufi saint Alauddin Ali Ahmed Sabir, known as Sabir Kaliyari. People of every faith come here — it's one of the oldest living symbols of Hindu–Muslim shared worship in the region. Second — Haridwar, just 30 km away: every evening at Har Ki Pauri, the Ganga aarti — fire, bells, and a river worshipped as a goddess. Roorkee's own canal is born from that same river at Haridwar's headworks. Third — the canal itself: in Roorkee you don't go to see water, you live with it — it's the town's promenade, its cooling system, its identity. The plains around it feed on canal irrigation — sugarcane and wheat country. And culturally? An engineering-and-education town with a cantonment heartbeat — you can feel it in the fest season, when IIT Roorkee's Cognizance, one of Asia's largest technical fests, takes over the town.

Extra context: local kitchens are classic western-Doab north-Indian — kachori–sabzi breakfasts, sweets like rabri and jalebi. Keep this as spoken colour, not a slide claim.

Transition: “From the plains, let's climb to where the mountains begin — Saloni takes over.”

Time: ~60s

## Slide 7 — Joshimath — Gateway to the Himalayas

**Presenters:** Saloni  ·  **Suggested time:** ~55s

SALONI: Thank you, Om — and now, welcome to my town. After Om's flat plains, feel the air thin: Joshimath sits at about 1,890 metres — nearly six times the elevation of Roorkee — in Chamoli district, built on a spur above the Alaknanda valley. Geography here is destiny. Just downstream, at Vishnuprayag, the Dhauliganga river merges with the Alaknanda — a confluence you can watch from the road. Everything about Joshimath is a gateway: it is the last major town on the highway to Badrinath, the base for Auli's ski slopes, and the roadhead for treks to the Valley of Flowers and Hemkund Sahib. In winter, when snow seals the high passes, Joshimath is where mountain life retires to — and it stays busy all year, not just in pilgrimage season.

Extra context: Rishikesh is roughly 250 km away by road; Joshimath's population is small (tens of thousands) but it swells enormously during the Char Dham season. Mention that the town literally cannot expand outward — it's bounded by steep slopes.

Transition: “But Joshimath is not only a gateway — it is one of the holiest addresses in the Himalaya.”

Time: ~55s

## Slide 8 — Joshimath — Ancient Heritage & Spiritual Identity

**Presenters:** Saloni  ·  **Suggested time:** ~60s

SALONI: Joshimath's older name is Jyotirmath — 'the monastery of light'. In the 8th century, the philosopher Adi Shankaracharya is credited with founding four cardinal monasteries in the four corners of India — Sringeri in the south, Puri in the east, Dwarka in the west, and Jyotirmath here in the north. Joshimath is the northern one. The Narsingh Temple, dedicated to Lord Vishnu's man-lion form, is the town's spiritual heart — and here is the detail I love most: every winter, when snow closes the Badrinath temple, the idol of Lord Badri is brought down and enthroned at Narsingh Temple. For roughly six months, Joshimath *is* Badrinath's seat. The town also holds the Kalpavriksha — an ancient tree said to be over a thousand years old, linked by tradition to Shankaracharya himself. Around all of this lives Garhwali culture — the language, the mountain villages, a rhythm of life shaped by pilgrimage seasons.

Extra context: temple-age figures (~1,200 years for the idol/tree) are traditional claims, not archaeologically fixed dates — phrase them as 'according to tradition'. Winter transfer of the idol happens around Diwali (Nov) and the deity returns to Badrinath in spring (Apr/May).

Transition: “And from this one town, an entire Himalayan universe opens up.”

Time: ~60s

## Slide 9 — Joshimath — Beyond the Town (Destinations)

**Presenters:** Saloni  ·  **Suggested time:** ~65s

SALONI: Joshimath is the doorway, and here's what lies beyond — but note the distances and seasons, because the Himalaya doesn't do 'nearby' the way plains do. Auli is genuinely close — about 14 km by road, or better: ride the ropeway. It's one of India's highest ski resorts, with meadows called bugyals and views of Nanda Devi. Vishnuprayag is just 10 km downstream — the river confluence. Badrinath, one of the four Char Dham shrines, is about 45 km — and it's seasonal: the temple opens roughly late April to early November. For the Valley of Flowers — a UNESCO World Heritage Site — you drive to Govindghat, about 20 km, then trek roughly 17 km to the valley; it's a July–September experience. Hemkund Sahib, the high-altitude Sikh shrine at ~4,630 m, shares the same trail up to Ghangaria and then climbs higher — also open only in summer. And Tapovan, across the river, offers hot springs and meadows. Same valley, very different calendars.

Extra context: ropeway is the Joshimath–Auli passenger ropeway. Distances are approximate road distances; trek distances are one-way. If asked about Nanda Devi National Park, it adjoins the Valley of Flowers.

Transition: “But in January 2023, my town made global news for a reason no one wanted.”

Time: ~65s

## Slide 10 — Joshimath — When the Mountains Send a Warning

**Presenters:** Saloni  ·  **Suggested time:** ~75s

SALONI: I want to present this carefully, because real families live this story. Joshimath is built on an old landslide — loose debris sitting on a steep, fractured rock slope. That was known for decades: in 1976, the Mishra Committee warned that the town sat on unstable material and recommended better drainage and restrictions on heavy construction. Those warnings went largely unheeded. In satellite data, the town sank about 8.9 cm between April and November 2022 — then about 5.4 cm in just twelve days around New Year 2023. In January 2023, cracks spread through hundreds of buildings — official surveys recorded around 868 houses damaged — and many families had to leave their homes. What caused it? The geology and poor drainage and unplanned construction are not controversial. What IS disputed is the role of the 520 MW Tapovan-Vishnugad hydropower project: several government-appointed studies did not blame it, while many residents and some independent experts disagree — and the project has repeatedly been hit by water seepage; tunnelling was halted again in August 2026 after a cavity opened in the main tunnel. The state responded with a ₹1,658-crore recovery plan based on 'Build Back Better'. The lesson isn't 'stop development' — it's that the Himalaya has a carrying capacity, and planning must respect it.

Extra context: be measured in tone — no disaster imagery; acknowledge displaced families. Distinguish clearly: (1) established: geology, drainage, construction on slopes; (2) disputed: hydropower project's contribution. Recovery plan approved 2023; implementation has been slow and is ongoing.

Transition: “So — one city asked nature to move; the other is being reminded that nature doesn't move. Let's put them side by side.”

Time: ~75s

## Slide 11 — Two Cities, Two Relationships with Nature

**Presenters:** Om + Saloni  ·  **Suggested time:** ~60s

OM: Let's put our two cities side by side, because they are almost mirror images. In Roorkee, people decided water should serve them — so they canalised the Ganga, hung a canal above a river, and built institutions on top of that engineering: irrigation, agriculture, a college, a cantonment, a university. Nature as something to be harnessed — with skill, and with consequences that took decades to understand. SALONI: And in Joshimath, people accepted that the mountain sets the rules — faith on a fragile slope, a tourist economy that arrives on nature's calendar, and now a subsidence crisis that is really nature reminding everyone of limits engineers can't draw on a map. TOGETHER: One city moved a river; the other must move carefully. The shared lesson: development and nature must coexist — whether you engineer a river or worship a mountain. Neither city is the 'wrong' model — they are two halves of the same state's experience.

Extra context: hand the slide over naturally — Om takes the left column, Saloni the right; both deliver the closing lesson line together if comfortable.

Transition: “So finally — let us each answer one simple question, personally.”

Time: ~60s

## Slide 12 — What Makes Our Cities Special?

**Presenters:** Om + Saloni  ·  **Suggested time:** ~55s

OM (reads his card, then speaks freely): What makes my city special is that it answers a question other cities ask consultants — 'where does your water come from?' — with its whole identity. In Roorkee, the water taught people engineering. I've walked along the canal in the evening — it's the town's living room. (Students may add one authentic personal line of their own here.) BOTH (together): Plains or peaks — our cities are both stories of Uttarakhand: people who made a life between the river and the sky.
